How to photograph your part

Your phone is fine. You don’t need an app, a light box, or a good camera — ten minutes of technique beats better equipment every time. Work through the list below and I’ll have everything I need to model your part accurately the first time.

10 minutes, start to finish

Set up first

Four things before you take a single photo. These matter more than any of the individual shots.

Step back and zoom in

Stand about three feet away and use your phone’s 2x or 3x zoom to fill the frame. Don’t hold the phone six inches from the part.

Up close, the wide lens stretches whatever is nearest to it. A close photo of a round knob makes the near edge read a few millimeters too big — and I’d print it that way.

Put a ruler in every shot

Lay a ruler or tape measure flat next to the part, in the same plane, with the markings readable. A quarter works in a pinch.

Photos have no size. The ruler is what lets me pull real dimensions off the image instead of guessing proportions.

Plain background, soft light

A sheet of paper, a clean bench, a cutting board — solid color, matte, and a different color than the part. No flash. Shade or a cloudy window beats direct sun.

Hard shadows read as edges. A dark shadow along a curve can look exactly like a step in the part.

Shoot straight on, not from an angle

Hold the phone flat and square to the face you’re shooting — parallel to it, pointed straight at the middle. Get above it and look down, don’t lean over it.

A tilted photo skews the shape. Square-on shots trace directly into CAD; angled ones have to be corrected by hand and lose accuracy.

The eight shots

Tap each one as you go. The first six are the part by itself from every side. The last two are the ones people forget, and they’re usually the ones that decide whether it fits.

0 of 8 done

Three numbers worth more than fifty photos

If you have calipers, use them and give me thousandths. A tape measure or ruler is fine too — just tell me which you used so I know how much to trust the number.

Overall length

End to end, the longest direction.

Width or diameter

Across the widest part. For round pieces, measure twice at 90° and tell me if it isn’t the same both ways.

The fit dimension

The shaft hole, the thread size, the slot width — whatever mates with something else. This is the one I can’t guess.

If your part is one of these

Shiny, chrome, black, or clear
These photograph badly — the camera sees reflections instead of edges. Dust it lightly with baby powder, flour, or cornstarch and blow off the excess. It brushes right off afterward. For clear parts, a strip of masking tape on the surface works too.
Smaller than a nickel
Phone cameras can’t focus that close without going soft. Take the photos anyway, but this is a part I’d rather have in my hand.
It has to snap, thread, or press into place
Photos capture shape well and tolerance badly. If your part has to click into a socket or thread onto something, mail it to me. A photo-only version will look right and fit wrong, and neither of us wants that round trip.
Completely gone — you have nothing to photograph
Still workable. Send photos of the empty space it mounts into with a ruler across it, any matching parts that survived, and a photo from a catalog, forum post, or another car. I’ve built parts from less.

Or skip all of this and mail me the part

Photos are for when you can’t part with it or it’s still installed. If you can live without it for a week, sending it is faster for you and better for the result — I measure it myself with calipers, and there’s nothing left to guess.

  • Wrap it, box it, no special packaging needed
  • You get the original back with the new one
  • Message me first and I’ll send the address

This is the right call for anything that has to fit precisely.

When you send them

  • Send the original photos, full size
  • Email works best for big batches — text is fine for a few
  • Tell me what the part goes to: make, model, and year if it’s a vehicle or appliance
  • Don’t screenshot your photos — it throws away most of the detail
  • Don’t crop, rotate, filter, or mark them up
  • Don’t use portrait mode — it blurs the background and sometimes the edges of the part

Short on time? A slow 30-second video circling the part, with a ruler next to it, gets me most of the way there. Move steadily and keep the part in the middle of the frame.
